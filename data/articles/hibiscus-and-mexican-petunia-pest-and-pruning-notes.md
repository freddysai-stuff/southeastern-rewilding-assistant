# Hibiscus & Mexican Petunia — Pest Watch and Pruning Notes for Coastal Summer Bloom

## Tropical Hibiscus (Hibiscus rosa-sinensis)

### Pruning

Light tip pruning after each bloom flush encourages branching and new flower
bud development. Remove spent blooms promptly (deadheading) so energy
doesn't go into seed production. If a stem goes woody and unproductive, cut
back by one-third to force fresh growth. Avoid heavy/reshaping pruning
mid-season — save significant cuts for after the summer bloom program ends.

### Pests to watch

- **Aphids** — cluster on new growth and flower buds. Knock off with a
  strong stream of water, or treat early with neem oil in the early morning.
- **Spider mites** — stippled, bronzed, or dull leaves, often with fine
  webbing underneath; more common during dry spells. Neem oil plus
  increased humidity (misting nearby ground) helps suppress outbreaks.
- **Whitefly** — tiny white insects that flutter when foliage is disturbed.
  Treat with neem oil or insecticidal soap at first sign; yellow sticky
  traps help with monitoring.

### Signs of a healthy, program-responsive plant

Deep glossy green foliage with no interveinal yellowing, multiple
simultaneous buds at various development stages, saturated flower color
lasting a full day, and steady new growth at branch tips.

## Mexican Petunia (Ruellia simplex)

### Pruning

Deadhead spent flowers regularly for a clean appearance and continuous bud
formation. If plants become leggy/floppy mid-season (common by July), cut
back hard to 6-8 inches above ground — this looks dramatic but the plant
rebounds within 2-3 weeks with a fresh full bloom flush. Timed mid-July,
this sets up peak performance for the final program weeks.

### Pests

Generally pest-resistant. Occasional leaf miners leave winding, pale trails
through leaf tissue — damage is primarily cosmetic; remove and dispose of
affected leaves rather than spraying.

### Spreading habit — manage it, use it

Mexican Petunia is a vigorous spreader and naturalizes in some Georgia
coastal wetlands and marsh edges, so treat it as **manageable rather than
avoid-entirely**: use physical edging barriers to control lateral spread in
garden beds, or grow in containers to prevent self-seeding/rhizome spread.
Deadheading before seed pods form also limits naturalization. In exchange
for that management effort, it delivers real value — near-continuous purple
bloom through the hottest months, strong pollinator/hummingbird forage, and
a fast, drought-tolerant groundcover that fills bare sandy soil and
outcompetes true weeds. Best suited to a contained/edged ornamental bed
rather than a naturalized restoration planting.
