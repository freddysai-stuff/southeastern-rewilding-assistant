# Budget Container Choices — Plastic vs. Terracotta, Repurposed Buckets & Fabric Grow Bags

## Plastic pots: the budget-friendly default

Plastic retains moisture far better than clay/terracotta, meaning less
frequent watering and lower cost over a season. It's also lightweight,
which matters for moving tropicals indoors before frost. Because plastic
doesn't "breathe," adjust watering technique rather than switching
material: stick a finger into the soil to the second knuckle - if damp and
cool, hold off; if dry to the fingertip, water thoroughly.

Many inexpensive plastic pots ship with sealed or only partially-punched
drainage holes. Before filling with soil, flip the pot over and use a
screwdriver, utility knife, or heated nail to fully open every hole - a pot
without real drainage will drown and rot roots within about two weeks
regardless of the plant.

Zero-cost heat/moisture tricks: prop plastic pots up on flat rocks or scrap
wood so they aren't sitting directly on hot concrete or decking (roots can
literally cook through the plastic on contact), and scatter a thin layer of
free mulch (dried grass clippings, fallen leaves, pine needles) over the
soil surface to reduce watering frequency further.

## Repurposing chlorine/pool-chemical buckets as pots

Old chlorine puck buckets are usually thick, heavy-duty #2 HDPE plastic
that outlasts cheap store pots - but they must be decontaminated before use
since plastic is porous and can leach residual chemical salts into soil,
scorching roots.

1. Wash thoroughly with dish soap, hot water, and a scrub brush to remove
   all visible residue.
2. Rinse completely with plain hose water.
3. Leave the buckets upside down in direct, blazing summer sun for 24-48
   hours - UV breaks down remaining chlorine. If any pool odor lingers,
   repeat for another day.
4. Drill drainage holes only after this cleaning process, sized and placed
   to match the intended plant (a few holes in the bottom only for
   moisture-loving plants; holes in the bottom *and* lower side walls, plus
   a false bottom of crushed cans/bottles/pinecones, for dry-soil natives
   that need extra drainage).

## Fabric grow bags

Fabric grow bags "air-prune" roots (air flows through the sidewalls),
preventing roots from circling the bottom and rotting the way they can in
an oversized solid pot. The tradeoff is that fabric dries out noticeably
faster than plastic, so heavier watering frequency is needed, and bags
should sit on a tray or flat rocks rather than directly on hot pavement.
Two plants sharing one larger bag (spaced 6-8 in. apart) merge into a
single bushier specimen faster than keeping them in separate bags - useful
when the goal is maximizing cutting material rather than a tidy solo
display.
