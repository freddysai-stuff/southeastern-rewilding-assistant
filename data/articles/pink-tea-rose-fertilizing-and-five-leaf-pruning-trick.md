# Pink Tea Rose — Low-Pain Deadheading, Feeding, and Fungal-Disease Prevention

## The "five-leaf-leaflet" deadheading trick

A full painful structural prune isn't necessary mid-summer to keep a rose
producing new blooms. Instead:

1. When a bloom fades and browns, follow the flower stem down until finding
   the first leaf cluster with **5 leaflets** (not 3).
2. Snip the stem about 1/4 in. above that 5-leaf cluster.
3. Roses will not push new flowers from a 3-leaflet node - cutting above a
   5-leaflet cluster signals the plant to send a fresh cane loaded with new
   buds.

Cheap leather welding gloves (rather than pricier "gardening" gloves) give
full forearm coverage against thorns for low-effort maintenance without the
expense of specialty rose gauntlets.

## Sun exposure and disease

Morning sun with afternoon shade is ideal - it dries overnight dew off
foliage quickly, which is one of the biggest natural preventers of fungal
disease like black spot. When feeding, always keep liquid fertilizer off
leaves and open petals (soil-only application) to avoid encouraging fungal
issues, and never feed a thirsty rose - soak the soil with plain water
first, then apply fertilizer, to prevent root/salt scorch.

## Feeding schedule (in-ground)

A high-phosphorus bloom booster (see the miracle-gro-bloom-booster-10-52-10
recipe) can be used once every 4 weeks through summer at roughly 2 tbsp per
2 gallons of water poured around the drip line - more than a potted plant
can take, since in-ground roses have far more root volume to buffer the
dose. Continuous heavy-phosphorus use without rotation risks a buildup that
locks up iron/zinc and yellows the leaves (chlorosis), so alternate with a
slower, balanced organic feed for everyday maintenance.

## Companion planting note

Wild/volunteer Mexican Petunia and self-seeding Rose Campion growing in the
same bed as roses is a low-cost, workable cottage-garden combination - both
tolerate the same phosphorus boost schedule and morning-sun exposure, and
Rose Campion's tall magenta spikes pair well with roses in a cut-flower
vase.
