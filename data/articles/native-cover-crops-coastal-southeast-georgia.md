# Native Cover Crops for Soil Restoration in Coastal Southeast Georgia

For soil restoration using regionally native species (rather than
agricultural cover crops), three species anchor a coastal Southeast Georgia
plan: Partridge Pea, Switchgrass, and Carolina Clover. All three thrive in
the region's sandy, humid, acidic coastal soils while building organic
matter, fixing nitrogen, and preventing wind/water erosion.

## The three anchor species

### Partridge Pea (Chamaecrista fasciculata)

Native spring/summer legume that rapidly fixes atmospheric nitrogen and
acts as a pioneer species in disturbed, degraded soils. Highly tolerant of
sandy, infertile, drought-prone soils, and self-seeds readily. Produces
extrafloral nectaries (nectar glands at the leaf base) that draw large
numbers of native bees, butterflies, and beneficial predatory insects.

### Switchgrass (Panicum virgatum)

A sturdy, deep-rooted perennial warm-season grass that builds immense
organic matter and adds heavy carbon to depleted soil. Naturally endemic to
the Southeastern Coastal Plain — resilient against heat, salt spray, and
sandy compaction.

### Carolina Clover (Trifolium carolinianum)

A native winter annual legume that fixes nitrogen while providing pollinator
forage. Unlike non-native clovers (e.g. Crimson Clover), it's adapted
specifically to the mild, wet winters and sandy soils of the Georgia coast —
though bulk seed sourcing is difficult (see the winter clover choices
article for the practical tradeoff against Crimson Clover).

## Seasonal planting windows

- **Summer (May-August)**: establish Partridge Pea and Switchgrass to
  outcompete weeds and build root mass during the intense heat.
- **Winter (October-December)**: switch to Carolina Clover, or a native
  grass like Eastern Gamagrass, to protect topsoil from winter erosion.

## Regional resources

For exact county-level soil deficiencies and warm-season biomass yields /
seeding rates, consult the University of Georgia Extension and the USDA
Plant Materials Center.
