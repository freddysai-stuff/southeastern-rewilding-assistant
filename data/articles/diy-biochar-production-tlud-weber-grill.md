# Making Your Own Biochar — TLUD Method in a Weber Grill

A standard kettle grill (e.g. Weber) can be used as a Top-Lit Updraft (TLUD)
pyrolyzer to make your own biochar from yard wood waste for free, instead of
buying bagged biochar. This is the production step that feeds into the
existing biochar recipes (`biochar-soap-molasses-soil-detox`,
`biochar-maintenance-broadcast`) and the charging recipe below
(`biochar-charging-tea-compost-worm-castings`) — those assume you already
have biochar on hand; this covers making it.

## Wood selection

- **Dense hardwood (oak, crepe myrtle):** cut into uniform 2-4 inch chunks,
  bone dry. Provides the structure for high-quality, long-lasting char.
- **Fast-burning softwood (pine):** used as kindling on top to get the burn
  going quickly.

## Phase 1 — Building and lighting the TLUD fire

1. Layer dense oak/crepe myrtle chunks at the very bottom of the grill bowl,
   packed tightly to minimize air gaps.
2. Layer fast-burning pine directly on top of the hardwood.
3. Light the **top** pine layer only. This is what makes it "top-lit
   updraft" — the top flame burns off the smoky volatile gases rising from
   the wood below, cooking the lower wood in an oxygen-starved environment
   (pyrolysis) without burning it to ash.
4. **Keep the bottom vent fully open** during the active burn, and **leave
   the lid off**. This feels counterintuitive (you want to starve the wood
   of oxygen) but is essential:
   - The open bottom vent feeds the draft that pushes flames upward,
     creating a restricted-oxygen zone at the bottom where the dense wood
     bakes into carbon instead of burning away.
   - Putting the lid on too early traps wood moisture, drops the internal
     temperature, and stalls the burn.
   - A closed lid also suffocates the top flame, which needs to keep
     roaring to consume the smoky gases below — without it you get
     billowing acrid yellow-white smoke instead of a clean burn.
5. Let the fire burn downward, unattended lid-off, until the wood turns
   completely black and ash-free — glowing red coals with no yellow flames.

## Phase 2 — Quenching (wet quench recommended)

**Wet quenching** (dousing with a hose while the lid is off) is generally
preferred over dry quenching (sealing the lid and vents to smother the
fire) because the sudden thermal shock fractures the char, creating
millions of microscopic cracks that make it both easier to crush and far
more absorbent for charging tea later.

Safe wet-quench process:
- Stand back at arm's length and use a hose with a spray nozzle — pouring
  water directly over hot coals produces a sudden, scalding steam blast.
- Keep spraying until the water level fully submerges the coals; wood
  coals hide hidden hot pockets, and spraying only the top lets the bottom
  keep smoldering into useless ash.
- Listen for loud crackling/hissing — that's water forcing into the
  carbon's pores, which is exactly what you want.
- Let it cool 10-15 minutes, then tip the grill to drain excess water
  before dumping the char out to crush.

(If you must dry-quench instead: wait until all wood is glowing red with no
yellow flames, close the lid and all vents completely, and leave sealed at
least 2-3 hours until fully cold. Wet quench is still the better choice
when a hose is available — the wet char also absorbs charging tea better
via osmosis once soaked.)

## Phase 3 — Crushing

Dump the cooled, drained char into a cart or tub and crush it down to
coarse-gravel/pea-gravel size using a heavy flat tool (a flat shovel or a
4x4 post works well). Damp char crushes more easily and produces far less
airborne carbon dust than fully dry char.

## Applying finished, charged biochar

- **Lawn areas:** broadcast charged biochar directly onto the ground and
  lightly rake it into the top inch before sowing cover crop seed.
- **Flower beds:** dig charged biochar directly into planting holes, or
  till it into the top 4-6 inches of the bed rather than surface-broadcasting.
  Beds generally need less structural disruption than a full lawn
  transformation, and incorporated biochar will hold moisture and
  significantly reduce summer watering needs.
- **General blend ratio:** roughly 10% biochar to 90% soil by volume for
  garden beds, containers, or vegetable patches. Because biochar is
  effectively permanent in the soil, it houses microbes, retains moisture,
  and holds nutrients for decades once charged.

**Always charge raw biochar before adding it to soil** — see
`biochar-charging-tea-compost-worm-castings` for the full charging/curing
recipe. Uncharged biochar acts like an empty sponge that temporarily steals
nutrients and moisture from surrounding soil instead of feeding it.
